/ The context
You're joining the AI Engineering team at BIT Capital, a tech-focused investment fund. We invest in
technology companies, including those building frontier AI, and our edge depends on staying at the
frontier of what the labs are actually doing, often before it shows up in a product or a stock price.

The people who build frontier AI (researchers and engineers at OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind,
Meta, xAI, Mistral, DeepSeek, Qwen, and the stealth startups they keep leaving to found) broadcast a
significant amount of signal in public: papers, technical blogs, GitHub, conference talks, and especially X.
So do the labs themselves, through official research blogs, model and system cards, release notes, and
their org accounts. Buried in that firehose are genuinely valuable, early signals: a capability break‐
through months before it's productized, a key researcher quietly moving to a competitor, a shift in a lab's
strategic direction, an architectural innovation to improve training or inference efficiencies with
ramifications throughout the semiconductor value chain, etc.

We want you to build the system that tracks this.

Before you write any code, research BIT Capital using whatever is public: our website, fund documents,
press, LinkedIn. Understand what we invest in and who would actually use this. Part of the task is figuring
out why this intelligence matters to a fund like us, and who the system users are.


/ Your task
Build a working system that:

  1. Tracks a register of who and what to follow: the frontier labs themselves (via their official channels)
    and the key individuals inside them.
 2. Ingests and extracts intelligence from what they publish: official lab blogs and accounts, plus
    individuals' papers, posts, repos, talks etc.
 3. Scores and ranks the contributors and the insights: this is the data-science core, and it should be
    justified and reproducible.
 4. Delivers actionable intelligence as reports and alerts to two internal audiences (see below).

/ What success means
We are not looking for a vibe-coded demo that ticks the boxes. We are looking for a system that actually
delivers interesting, relevant intelligence with good filtering, one that shows taste and gets the details
right. The single most important question we'll ask when we open your submission is: did this surface
something we'd genuinely want to know, and did it keep the noise out? Build toward that.


/ The two audiences
The same raw intelligence serves two internal consumers who care about overlapping but different
things. Build one shared core (ingestion → register → extraction → scoring) and two tailored last-mile out‐
puts:

  • Investment team (PMs / analysts): "what does this mean for our positions?" e.g. a star researcher
    leaving a lab to found a startup; a capability jump that threatens a holding's moat or validates a thesis;
    shifts in the competitive map between the public names exposed to AI, implications for the semicon‐
    ductor and energy supply chains. Framing: implications, tickers, theses. (Note: most labs are private,
    so part of the judgment is connecting lab developments to where they actually land for a public-
    equity investor.)
  • AI team (the people you'd be joining): "what should we adopt or investigate?" e.g. a new agentic-or‐
    chestration or evaluation technique to bring into our own platform; a cheaper/better open model; a
    paper that changes how we'd build a pipeline. Framing: technical, build-relevant.

We want to see that you understand the difference and tailor the output, not that you build two separate
systems.


/ The system, part by part
1 Frame the problem (who & what to track)
Decide which labs and which individuals matter, and why. How do you define "key"? Founders and re‐
search leads are obvious; the real signal is often one layer down, and a lot of it comes straight from the
labs' own channels. How do you keep the register current as people move and new names emerge?

2 Ingestion pipeline
Pull from heterogeneous sources on a schedule. Official lab channels (blogs, org X accounts, etc.), papers
(e.g. arXiv), GitHub, and more. The sources are messy and some are gated or expensive. We care about
how you handle source selection, rate limits, deduplication, and freshness, plus how you reason about
which sources are worth the effort. Decide and justify your coverage: a thoughtfully-scoped set of
sources done well beats a broad set done badly.

3 The register of labs and individuals
This is the foundation: everything downstream is only as good as who and what you choose to track.
Track two kinds of entity:

  • The labs themselves, as first-class entities, via their official channels (research blog, org X account,
    publications/research index, GitHub org, model and system cards, newsroom). A lot of the highest-
    value signal is published by the lab, not a named person, so tracking individuals alone will miss
    releases, system cards, and official announcements entirely.
  • The key individuals inside them: the obvious founders and research leads, but also the layer below
    them where much of the real signal sits.

Get the right labs and people, keep the list current as people move and new names and orgs emerge, and
do the entity resolution to know that an X handle, an arXiv author name, and a GitHub account are the
same person (and that a personal post and an official lab post are different kinds of source). Part
automated discovery, part judgment.

4 Extraction & intelligence
For each tracked entity, lab or individual, extract the key contributions and developments over a recent
window (you choose it, we'd suggest ~3 months; recency and trend matter). Turn raw content into struc‐
tured, attributed insights (attributed to the person or the lab), with the LLM doing the heavy lifting. Every
insight must be traceable to its primary source: cite cleanly.

5 Scoring & rating: the data-science part
Score and rate both contributors and insights. This is where we look hardest at your data-science
instincts. A score is only as good as its justification: what are the inputs, why those weights, how do you
know it's measuring what you claim? Validate it, against ground truth, human judgment, or a defensible
proxy. An arbitrary weighted sum dressed up as a "score" is a red flag; a simple model you can defend
and have tested is exactly what we want.

6 Signal vs. noise: the most important part
Most of the firehose is noise. The system must surface the genuinely important, novel, and actionable,
and suppress everything else. This is where taste shows, and it's weighted accordingly. Show us your
filtering logic and your judgment.

7 Actionable intelligence: reports + alerts
Deliver the goods to both audiences:

  • Reports: a periodic digest (you choose the cadence) readable in the app and exportable as PDF.
  • Alerts: when something material appears, push it (e.g. Slack/email) to the right audience.

Both must cite sources and be tailored per persona (see above). "Actionable" means a reader knows what
it means and what to do, not just a summary of what happened.

8 Web interface
A small surface where a user can browse the register, see the scored insights and why they were flagged,
read past reports, and configure what's tracked. Keep it light and quick. Don't spend too much time
polishing the UI.

/ How to prioritize
The case study is deliberately ambitious. We've set a high ceiling on purpose, because we want to see
how far you can take it. Aim to complete it all — but know that if you don't, that's not automatically a
failure. In that case, we prefer to see a few sections done in depth, rather than all sections done
superficially in an attempt to "tick all boxes". To help you prioritize, we are providing the table below. It's
a rough ranking of what's important for us (in descending order). We suggest that you start at the top
and work your way down.

/ Deliverables: what we'll be looking at
  • Source code in a Git repo: clear, modular, good primitives (clean functions for ingestion, extraction,
    scoring, storage, orchestration, delivery). README + a demo we can run locally (or a live deployment).
  • The database: schema and real data.
  • Architecture write-up: the stack and why, including model selection per task (which model for which
    job, and why) and fallback strategies.
  • Prompts with your design rationale.
  • Evaluation: how you measured extraction quality and controlled hallucination, and how you validated
    the scoring. Show your ground-truth approach.
  • Tokenomics: approximate token usage and $ cost per workflow, and how cost shaped your model
    choices (cost-quality trade-offs).
  • Final report: what works, what you'd do next, what you learned, and the 3–5 most interesting real
    insights your system actually surfaced (this is your proof that it works).
  • Optional: a short Loom / video demo.
